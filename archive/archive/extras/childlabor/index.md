# The Children Who Went to School

In the summer of 1833,
a delegation of mill owners from the English Midlands appeared before Parliament
to explain why they should be allowed to keep working children under nine years old
in their textile factories.
The arguments they made would be familiar to anyone who has followed a debate
about labor standards in the last two hundred years.
Families in poor districts depended on their children's wages to survive.
Interfering in private contracts between employers and workers
was an overreach of government authority.
And if Parliament pushed too hard,
manufacturers would simply move their operations elsewhere.

Parliament passed the Factory Act anyway.
Children under nine could no longer work in textile mills.
Children under thirteen were limited to nine hours of work per day.
The act created four paid factory inspectors
to cover the whole of England, Wales, and Scotland.
Four inspectors for a country with thousands of mills.
The mill owners probably thought they had won.

They had not.

## The Inspection Mechanism

The inspectors' early authority was limited.
They could not enter a factory without the owner's permission,
which meant that inspections happened when owners were prepared for them,
which meant the inspections were not terribly informative.
This was corrected.
The inspectors were given the right of entry,
and their reports began to document what the mills actually looked like
on an ordinary working day.

Those reports created a paper trail.
The paper trail produced fines.
The fines made compliance cheaper than defiance,
at least for most owners most of the time.
This is not a complicated mechanism.
It is the same mechanism used to enforce building codes,
food safety standards,
and traffic laws.
The novelty was applying it to child labor,
against the vigorous objections of people
who had every financial reason to prefer the previous arrangement.

The mechanism worked slowly.
By 1878, no child under ten could work in any factory in England.
By 1901, the minimum age was twelve.
Progress measured in decades is frustrating,
but it is worth noting that the people making the original arguments in 1833
had predicted the entire textile industry would relocate.
It did not.
The predicted catastrophe failed to materialize,
as predicted catastrophes in labor reform debates have a long habit of doing.

## From One Country to One Hundred and Eighty

The British example spread through colonial administration,
through trade competition,
and through the growing conviction
that children who worked in factories were children who could not attend school.
Compulsory schooling laws reinforced the labor restrictions:
if a child was legally required to be in a classroom,
it became harder to keep them on a factory floor.
The two mechanisms worked together.

The International Labour Organization was founded in 1919
as part of the settlement that ended the First World War.
Its founding constitution committed member states to a living wage,
an eight-hour working day,
and an end to child labor.
The ILO's structure was unusual:
unlike most international bodies,
it included representatives of workers and employers alongside government delegates,
on the theory that labor standards were too important to leave to governments alone.

For most of its history,
the ILO's conventions were ratified by some countries and ignored by others.
Convention 182,
adopted in 1999 and covering the worst forms of child labor—slavery,
trafficking, forced labor,
and hazardous work—turned out to be different.
In 2020, Tonga became the last ILO member state to ratify it.
Every country in the organization had ratified the same convention.
This had never happened before with any international labor standard.
The mill owners' grandchildren had lost the argument entirely.

## Making School Worth Attending

Legal prohibition and inspection are necessary,
but they are not sufficient.
A family whose children's labor is the difference between eating and not eating
will not stop sending children to work because it has become illegal.
They will instead find ways around the law,
or move into sectors where inspection is weaker,
or face a choice that most people reading this have never had to face.

Brazil understood this when it launched the Bolsa Família program in 2003.
The program is straightforward:
the government transfers cash to poor families,
on the condition that their children attend school regularly
and receive their scheduled vaccinations.
The conditionality matters.
It changes the economic calculation for families
without pretending the calculation does not exist.
Studies found that Bolsa Família reduced child labor significantly
among participating families.
It did not eliminate it,
but it moved the numbers in a direction that two centuries of prohibition alone had not managed.

India's Midday Meal scheme took a different approach to the same problem.
Free school lunches provided a concrete incentive for enrollment
in communities where keeping a child in school
had previously meant forgoing a day's contribution to household income.
Attendance went up.
Dropout rates among children who might otherwise have been working went down.
Both the Brazilian and Indian programs recognized something the English factory inspectors
could not have acted on even if they had understood it:
that removing children from labor requires making school affordable for families,
not just illegal for employers.

### The Numbers

The ILO and UNICEF estimate that child labor fell from roughly 246 million children in 2000
to 160 million in 2020.
That is a large reduction,
and it happened because of the mechanisms described in this lesson:
legal standards, inspection, enforcement, and economic support for families.
The worst remaining concentrations are in sub-Saharan Africa
and in agriculture,
which is harder to inspect and regulate than factories.
A child picking cocoa in Côte d'Ivoire or harvesting cotton in Uzbekistan
is far less visible to inspectors than a child working in a textile mill.
The problem is not solved.
The trend is real,
and the mechanism is understood.

## The Same Arguments, Two Centuries Later

In 2021, a coalition of garment manufacturers argued before the United States Congress
that proposed supply chain legislation
would cause companies to move production to countries with less oversight.
In 2019, cocoa industry groups argued that stricter child labor requirements
for their supply chains would drive up prices
in ways that would hurt the poor families growing the cocoa.
In 2016, several major electronics manufacturers argued
that liability for labor conditions in their suppliers' factories
was impractical and would damage competitiveness.

These are the arguments the mill owners made in 1833.
They have been wrong,
in every country and every industry where the reform in question eventually happened,
for two hundred years.
This is not because companies are lying,
exactly,
though some of them are.
It is because the people making the arguments have a financial interest in the conclusion
and because predicting the future is genuinely hard
and because in any particular moment the costs of change are concrete and visible
while the benefits are abstract and distributed.

The evidence says otherwise.
Where inspection exists and penalties are real,
conditions improve.
Where companies face genuine liability for what happens in their supply chains,
conditions improve.
Where conditional cash transfers allow families to send their children to school,
child labor falls.
None of this is complicated.
The Factory Act of 1833 was not a complicated piece of legislation.
Four inspectors and a set of fines produced measurable change,
slowly,
against sustained opposition from people
who were absolutely certain the consequences would be catastrophic.

The mechanism is not the mystery.
The political will to apply it is what varies.
