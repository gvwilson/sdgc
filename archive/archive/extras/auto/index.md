# Big Tech is Like the Auto Industry

For the first six decades of the automobile era,
injuries and deaths from car crashes were attributed almost entirely to driver error.
The industry built this framing deliberately:
"the nut behind the wheel" was the problem, not the vehicle.
This was convenient for manufacturers,
who could sell safety as a marketing feature when it suited them
and disclaim responsibility for crashes when it did not.
"The nut behind the wheel" is the direct ancestor of
"users choose to see this content,"
"bad actors misuse our platform,"
and "parents should monitor their children's screen time."
The framing serves the same function in every case:
to locate the cause of harm in the individual user
and foreclose systemic solutions.

The engineering evidence for design-based safety was not absent in the early decades of mass motoring.
It was suppressed.
Crash reconstruction studies, hospital trauma data, and biomechanical research
all pointed toward the vehicle as a primary determinant of injury severity.
Industry lawyers and publicists worked systematically
to keep that evidence out of regulatory hearings and public discussion.
When researchers published inconvenient findings,
the manufacturers questioned their methodology.
When legislators proposed standards,
the industry warned of ruinous costs.

What made the "nut behind the wheel" frame so durable was that it contained enough truth to be defensible.
Drivers did make mistakes.
Alcohol was genuinely involved in a large fraction of fatal crashes.
These facts allowed the industry to concede the obvious
while redirecting attention away from the questions that would have cost it money:
why did a survivable impact so often become a fatal one,
and what could be built differently to change that?
The same rhetorical move appears in contemporary arguments about social media harm.
Users do choose to engage with content.
Parents do have some ability to monitor their children.
Acknowledging these things costs nothing
and forecloses the harder question of whether a system designed to maximize engagement
is a system designed to cause harm.

Beneath this framing lies a legal structure designed to disperse accountability.
[%g limited_liability "Limited liability" %] is not a natural feature of commercial life.
It is a legal instrument created by legislatures at specific historical moments
to encourage investment in risky enterprises
by protecting investors from losses beyond what they put in.
The New York Free Banking Act of 1838 and the United Kingdom's Limited Liability Act of 1855
were deliberate policy choices that severed the connection between ownership and responsibility.
The intended beneficiaries were investors and shareholders.
The people adjacent to a factory, or prescribed a drug whose dangers were known to the manufacturer,
were not the intended beneficiaries.
Limited liability shifted risk onto them while concentrating returns elsewhere.

[%g diffuse_decision_making "Diffuse decision-making" %] is the mechanism that limited liability enables
at the organizational level.
When a large corporation causes harm,
the investigation typically finds that many people made many small decisions,
none of which individually was the obvious cause,
and no single person authorized the outcome.
The Ford Pinto's fuel system was designed by engineers who met safety standards as they understood them,
reviewed by managers who weighed costs against projected liability,
and approved by executives managing portfolios of risk across a large organization.
The internal memo that became infamous calculated the cost of a recall
against the expected litigation costs of injury settlements,
a standard form of analysis in the industry.
No individual told an engineer to design a car that would kill people.
The outcome emerged from a process,
and the process was not anyone's direct decision.

Ralph Nader's contribution was not the discovery of automobile danger.
It was the documentation of specific, named defects in specific, named vehicles,
written in language a congressional hearing could act on.
His 1965 book focused on the Chevrolet Corvair's rear suspension design
and the industry's knowledge of its handling problems,
but the argument was general:
manufacturers knew about preventable deaths and had chosen not to prevent them.
General Motors responded by hiring private investigators to follow Nader,
tap his phone,
and find compromising information about him.
When this was exposed,
the resulting Senate hearings featured GM's president apologizing to Nader in public.
The National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act passed the same year, 1966,
with the authority to set mandatory federal safety standards for the first time.
The industry's attempt at intimidation had produced the legislation it was intended to prevent.

The fight over airbags illustrates how the industry continued to operate
even after it lost the first regulatory battle.
The technology was viable by the early 1970s.
Ford and GM both conducted internal studies confirming its effectiveness.
Neither moved to deploy it at scale,
and both lobbied actively against requirements to do so.
The arguments shifted over the decade:
airbags were too expensive, too unreliable, would cause injuries of their own,
were unnecessary if seatbelt use could be increased through other means.
Each argument bought time.
Mandatory airbag requirements did not take full effect in the United States until the 1990s.
By conservative estimates, tens of thousands of people died in the interval
who would have survived with airbag protection.
That lag was not accidental.

Organizational language develops specific forms for managing
the acknowledgment of harm without the acknowledgment of responsibility.
"Mistakes were made" is the exemplary construction: passive voice, no agent, no decision.
"We have learned lessons" implies a completed process of improvement
without specifying what went wrong or who was responsible for it.
"We take this very seriously" commits to nothing.
These formulas are not accidents of writing style.
They are the products of careful attention by communications and legal teams
to the exact threshold between acknowledgment, which can be useful for public relations,
and admission, which creates liability.
The language is optimized to produce the appearance of accountability while avoiding its substance.

Sweden's [%g vision_zero "Vision Zero" %] program, adopted in 1997,
represents a genuinely different starting point rather than a stricter version of the same approach.
Its premise is that road deaths are a system failure rather than an accumulation of individual failures.
People will always make mistakes while driving.
The question is whether the system is designed to make those mistakes lethal.
Under Vision Zero, the obligation to prevent death and serious injury
falls primarily on system designers, not road users.
This reframing has practical consequences:
it shifts investment toward road geometry, vehicle speed management,
and separation of pedestrians and cyclists from vehicle traffic,
rather than toward enforcement and education campaigns directed at individual behavior.
Sweden's fatality rate per billion kilometers traveled fell by more than half
in the two decades after Vision Zero's adoption.

Medicine and law provide a partial countermodel.
Physicians and lawyers are individually licensed, individually liable for malpractice,
and can lose their license to practice as a result of professional misconduct.
This creates a form of accountability that persists
even when the practitioner works within a large organization.
The resistance to extending similar professional liability frameworks
to software engineering and financial services
comes primarily from those industries,
which have successfully framed such proposals as unworkable or innovation-dampening.

The shift from "system failure" to "user error" as the preferred explanatory frame
for technology-related harm is a specific and consequential application of this pattern.
When software causes harm, the dominant response is to attribute the harm to misuse.
Terms of service agreements transfer responsibility to users.
Security breaches are attributed to users who clicked a link.
Financial losses from platform design are attributed to users who made bad decisions.
This framing is not simply wrong,
because user behavior is genuinely part of many failure chains.
But the deliberate selection of user error as the primary explanation,
in the face of evidence for system-level contributions,
is a political choice that benefits the party with the most control over the system
and the least accountability for its outcomes.
