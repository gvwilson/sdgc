# Automation and Who Pays for Progress

Every wave of labor-displacing technology has been accompanied by
confident predictions that it will create more jobs than it destroys,
and by a distribution of costs and benefits that has been highly
unequal. The workers whose skills are made redundant bear
concentrated, immediate costs; the benefits of increased productivity
are distributed broadly and delayed.  This asymmetry is not inherent
to technology. It is a consequence of the political economy in which
technology is developed and deployed. The history of mechanization
from the textile mills to the office computer offers a set of concrete
cases for evaluating current claims about AI and automation.

The [%g luddites "Luddites" %] did not object to technology as such. The popular image of them as irrational opponents of progress is a deliberate falsification that began during their own lifetime and has been repeated ever since because it is useful. What the Luddites actually objected to was the use of new machinery to circumvent the labor agreements, apprenticeship structures, and quality standards that skilled textile workers had built over generations. They were not opposed to new looms. They were opposed to the deployment of those looms by manufacturers who used them to replace skilled workers with unskilled workers earning lower wages, producing cheaper goods under worse conditions. Their critique was economic and political, not technological, and the historical evidence vindicates most of it.

Textile mechanization in Britain increased aggregate productivity substantially. It also produced decades of severe immiseration for the handloom weavers whose skills it made redundant. Weavers' real wages fell sharply and did not recover to pre-mechanization levels for a generation. The productivity gains were captured by manufacturers and by the consumers who purchased cheaper cloth. The workers whose skills were devalued received no share of those gains and bore the full cost of the transition. The eventual employment growth that came with expanded textile production employed workers at lower skill levels and lower wages than the workers it displaced. The people who suffered were not the same people who eventually benefited, and the timeline across which the benefits arrived was measured in decades, not years.

Agricultural mechanization in the twentieth century followed the same distributional logic. The introduction of combines, tractors, and mechanized harvesting eliminated the need for large seasonal agricultural labor forces across Europe and North America. Rural communities that had organized themselves around that labor supply lost their economic basis. The people displaced were sharecroppers, tenant farmers, and seasonal agricultural workers. They had no capital claim on the land being mechanized and no transferable skills that commanded premium wages elsewhere. The transition to industrial agriculture increased food production and reduced its cost. The rural communities that lost their labor markets did not share in those gains.

The feminization and subsequent elimination of clerical work is a case study in how automation's effects interact with existing social hierarchies. Clerical work in the nineteenth century was predominantly male and reasonably well-paid. As firms expanded and required more record-keeping, the demand for clerical labor increased faster than wages could attract sufficient men. Women were recruited into clerical roles at substantially lower wages. By the early twentieth century, clerical work had been feminized and its wages had declined relative to other occupations. Then, across the second half of the twentieth century, office automation, from accounting machines to computers, eliminated the routine clerical work that women had come to dominate. The workforce that bore the cost of the second transition was overwhelmingly the one that had been recruited at a discount to fill the gap in the first.

The historical record on predictions about jobs of the future is consistently poor. Every major wave of labor-displacing technology has been accompanied by confident claims that it will ultimately create more jobs than it destroys, that workers will be retrained, that new industries will absorb the displaced. Some of these claims have eventually proved true in aggregate. None of them has been true on the timeline relevant to the workers displaced. The gap between "true in aggregate over several decades" and "true for the specific workers and communities affected" is where the actual human cost lives.

The distribution of automation's benefits is not determined by the technology. It is determined by the political and legal conditions under which the technology is deployed. The same mechanization can produce different distributional outcomes depending on whether workers have collective bargaining rights, whether they have access to retraining programs, whether there are policy mechanisms to capture some of the productivity gains as public revenue, and whether the legal system gives workers any claim on the increased value their former employers extract. These are not questions technology can answer. They are questions that are answered by legislatures, courts, labor relations boards, and the relative power of workers and employers in each specific context.

AI occupies a peculiar position in this history because its proponents claim for it a qualitative difference from prior automation: it does not merely perform physical tasks or routine information processing, it performs tasks that require judgment, pattern recognition, and something that looks like expertise. This claim may be true in some domains. What is not different is the distributional logic. The companies deploying AI systems are not organized to share the productivity gains with the workers displaced, and there is no policy infrastructure currently in place that would require them to. The question worth asking about AI is not whether it will eliminate jobs, since it will eliminate some, but whether the conditions under which it is deployed will differ in any meaningful way from the conditions under which textile looms, agricultural machinery, and office computers were deployed. The historical record provides the baseline for that comparison.
