# Big Tech is Like a Hollywood Studio

Between roughly 1920 and 1948, five major studios controlled the
American film industry through vertical integration: they owned the
production facilities, employed the talent under exclusive long-term
contracts, controlled the distribution networks, and owned the theater
chains where films were exhibited. A performer, director, or writer
under studio contract could not work for a competing studio without
permission and could be suspended without pay for refusing a role.
The studio decided what films were made, who made them, how they were
distributed, and who saw them.

In 1948, the Supreme Court ruled in United States v. Paramount
Pictures that the studios' simultaneous ownership of production and
exhibition was an illegal restraint of trade requiring divestiture of
the theater chains. The Paramount case is the most direct legal
precedent for current arguments that platforms should not
simultaneously operate marketplaces and compete in them.

The studio talent contract system was not simply employment. A performer under contract was an asset on the studio's balance sheet. The studio owned the performer's screen name, controlled which roles they took, decided when they could work and for whom, and held the right to extend the contract unilaterally through the [%g option_clause "option clause" %]. A performer who refused an assigned role could be suspended without pay, and that suspension time was added to the end of the contract, preventing the performer from working for any competitor. The system transferred the economic value of a performer's public persona to the studio while placing all creative risk on the performer. Bette Davis challenged Warner Bros. over this system in 1936 and lost. Olivia de Havilland challenged it in 1943 and won a California court ruling that effectively limited personal service contracts to seven years. The human cost of the intervening decades was real and documented.

[%g block_booking "Block booking" %] was the distribution mechanism that completed the system's market control. Studios required theater owners who wanted access to a studio's most desirable films to purchase entire slates of films in advance, sight unseen. A theater owner who wanted one guaranteed hit had to accept a package of films including ones no audience would have paid to see. This practice, called block booking or [%g blind_selling "blind selling" %], foreclosed competition at the exhibition level by making it economically irrational for theater owners to seek films from smaller independent studios. The analog on contemporary platforms is the tying arrangement: a developer who wants access to iOS users must accept App Store review policies, in-app payment requirements, and commission structures as an inseparable package.

The Hays Code, formally the Motion Picture Production Code, was administered from 1934 onward by the Production Code Administration under Joseph Breen. It was a private standard with the force of an industry-wide ban: films without a PCA seal could not be exhibited in any major theater chain in the United States. The Code specified in granular detail what content was prohibited, from depictions of interracial romance to excessive violence, and what moral frameworks stories were required to endorse. It was not a government regulation; it was a private content moderation system enforced through distribution control. The parallel to contemporary platform content policies is structural. The platform sets the standards, adjudicates compliance, and controls access to the distribution channel that makes the business viable.

The Paramount case turned on a straightforward application of Sherman Act Section 1 and Section 2 principles. The government argued, and the Supreme Court agreed, that the studios' simultaneous ownership of production and exhibition, combined with block booking and other distribution practices, constituted an unlawful combination in restraint of trade. The remedy was divestiture: the studios were required to sell their theater chains. The case established that vertical integration across a distribution bottleneck, combined with practices that foreclose competition at adjacent market levels, is subject to antitrust remedy. It is the direct legal ancestor of current arguments that platforms should not simultaneously operate marketplaces and compete within them as sellers.

The post-Paramount film industry did not collapse. It changed. Independent producers gained access to theaters. A wider range of films could be made. Foreign films became commercially viable in the United States. New talent emerged outside the studio system. The industry restructured around a different set of relationships. This is not a perfect analogy for what platform divestiture might produce, because the technology platforms are more deeply integrated into their products than theater chains were into films. But the Paramount precedent does establish that market restructuring following divestiture can produce a more competitive landscape rather than simply destroying the industry.

What the studio system ultimately demonstrates is that control of distribution is more durable than control of production. The studios that lost their theater chains retained their production and distribution infrastructure and survived. The talent they had controlled under personal service contracts dispersed into a more competitive market. The studios adapted. This pattern recurs: the entity that controls the distribution channel can survive losing control of content production, because content producers need the distribution channel more than the channel needs any individual producer. Platform operators understand this. Their investments in distribution infrastructure, not content or product quality, are what makes their market position defensible.
