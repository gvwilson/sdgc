# Big Tech is Like a Drug Company

Before the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906,
companies sold remedies with names like Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup,
whose active ingredient was morphine,
and various "vegetable compounds" claiming to treat conditions from colds to cancer.
The [%g patent_medicine "patent medicine" %] industry operated on manufactured testimonials,
suppressed adverse evidence,
and advertising that linked products to health and vitality,
combined with aggressive lobbying against any regulatory proposal.

The pre-regulatory market rested on the principle that buyers were responsible for their own decisions.
In practice,
this meant that a seriously ill person, frightened and with limited access to medical care,
could spend money they did not have
on a product that contained alcohol, opium, cocaine, or nothing at all.
[%g caveat_emptor "Caveat emptor" %] works in markets where buyers can assess quality before purchase.
It fails systematically when the product is sold on medical claims the buyer cannot evaluate,
the harm may take years to appear,
and the buyer is in a state that compromises judgment.
The patent medicine market was not a demonstration that free markets work.
It was a demonstration of what markets produce
when information is profoundly asymmetric and the product exploits vulnerability.

The testimonial was the patent medicine industry's primary marketing technology.
Companies collected and published accounts from satisfied customers,
paid for endorsements from public figures,
and fabricated testimonials outright when real ones were insufficient.
The structural logic is identical to contemporary influencer marketing:
a social proof claim is more persuasive than a direct product claim
because it appears to come from a disinterested third party.

Newspapers that accepted patent medicine advertising did not publish critical coverage of patent medicines.
This was often not explicit editorial policy
but a structural consequence of financial dependence.
Some medicine companies included "poison clauses" in their advertising contracts
that voided the contract if the publication ran negative coverage
or supported regulatory legislation.
The relationship between advertising revenue and editorial independence this created
has a direct contemporary analog:
publications that depend on platform distribution and advertising for their survival
are structurally discouraged from publishing critical coverage of those platforms.

Samuel Hopkins Adams published "The Great American Fraud" in Collier's Weekly in 1905
as a series documenting specific harms from specific products,
naming the products, the companies, and the claims they made.
He obtained the products, had them chemically analyzed,
identified the active ingredients,
and compared what the labels claimed against what the products contained.
He documented deaths attributable to specific products.
The series was a model of accountability journalism
in the sense that it produced specific, verifiable claims that subjects could rebut if the claims were wrong.
They largely could not.

The Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 required accurate labeling of contents
but did not require that products demonstrate efficacy before being sold.
A company could continue selling a useless remedy
as long as it did not make false claims about ingredients.
This limitation proved consequential.
Regulatory adequacy tends to track demonstrable harm rather than anticipate it,
and the next disaster drove the next round of reform.

Thalidomide was developed by a West German company
and approved as a sedative in multiple European countries, Canada, and Australia
without the clinical testing that would later become standard.
It caused severe limb malformations in thousands of children born to mothers who took it during pregnancy.
The United States was largely spared because FDA examiner Frances Kelsey
repeatedly refused to approve the drug, citing insufficient safety data,
despite sustained pressure from the US distributor.
Kelsey's refusal was not the result of superior regulatory foresight.
It was the result of a licensing process that had not been compressed by political pressure
to match the speed of European approvals.
The thalidomide catastrophe revealed that regulatory variation between countries
was not merely an administrative inconvenience but a matter of life and serious harm,
and drove legislative reform in multiple countries simultaneously.

The Kefauver-Harris Amendment of 1962 required pharmaceutical companies
to demonstrate not just that their drugs were safe
but that they were effective for the conditions they claimed to treat.
This shifted the burden of proof:
before 1962, the FDA had to demonstrate harm to block a drug;
after 1962, the company had to demonstrate benefit to obtain approval.
The industry argued this would slow the introduction of beneficial drugs,
raise development costs,
and reduce innovation.
What is clear is that the pre-1962 standard had produced demonstrable catastrophes,
and that the industry's opposition to the reform was not primarily driven by concern for patients.

Purdue Pharma's conduct regarding OxyContin is the most thoroughly documented recent case
of a pharmaceutical company suppressing evidence of harm
and capturing the institutions responsible for constraining it.
Internal documents produced in litigation show that Purdue knew the drug was being diverted for non-medical use
and that its marketing claims about the addiction risk of sustained-release opioids were not supported by evidence.
The company paid high-prescribing physicians to serve as speakers at promotional events,
funded research through intermediaries designed to produce favorable results,
and trained its sales force to minimize addiction risk in conversations with prescribers.
The aggregate effect was an epidemic that killed hundreds of thousands of people
across the US, Canada, Australia, and the UK.
The legal resolution allowed the Sackler family to retain most of its wealth
through a bankruptcy settlement that immunized family members from civil suits.
The parallels to platform companies that have internal research showing their products cause harm,
suppress or minimize that research,
and lobby against regulation are not metaphorical.

The same playbook was exported across borders.
Purdue's marketing strategies were deployed in Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia,
where opioid prescribing rates rose sharply following the introduction of OxyContin in those markets,
using the same speaker programs and the same minimization of addiction risk.
This demonstrates that the tactics are portable:
they work in regulatory environments with different formal structures
because they target the underlying incentive systems those structures share.
Regulatory reform in a single country is insufficient
if the industry can simply shift its tactics to markets where the regulatory infrastructure is less developed.

Regulatory capture in the pharmaceutical context operates through several documented mechanisms.
The most direct is the revolving door:
regulators who expect to return to industry employment
have incentives to maintain working relationships with the firms they regulate,
and former regulators are valuable to those firms precisely because of their institutional knowledge.
The pharmaceutical industry also funds a majority of the clinical research on which drug approvals are based,
creating structural conflicts of interest managed through disclosure requirements
rather than through the independence of the research enterprise.
The industry funds the continuing medical education programs
through which practicing physicians learn about new drugs,
creating a system in which the entity selling the drug is also substantially responsible for training the prescribers.
All of these mechanisms are currently active in the context of platform regulation.

The [%g trips "TRIPS agreement" %],
the intellectual property chapter of the World Trade Organization framework negotiated in 1994,
extended pharmaceutical patent protections globally
and required member countries to enforce those protections as a condition of WTO membership.
The practical consequence was that people in countries
without the resources to pay patent-protected prices for life-saving drugs
died of diseases for which effective treatments existed.
The Doha Declaration of 2001 established that countries facing public health emergencies
could issue compulsory licenses to manufacture generics,
but the mechanisms have been contested by pharmaceutical companies
in subsequent trade negotiations and investment treaty arbitrations.
