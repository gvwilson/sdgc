# Big Tech is Like the East India Company

The expansion of extractive relationships between wealthy and poor
regions did not end with formal decolonization. The structural
features of classical colonialism have reappeared in new forms:
resource extraction, one-sided infrastructure, governance that serves
the metropole rather than the colony, and the systematic suppression
of local alternatives. Understanding those classical features is
necessary for recognizing their current versions. The East India
Company, King Leopold's Congo Free State, and the cotton plantation
systems of the 18th and 19th centuries are not ancient history: they
are the template.

The English East India Company was chartered in 1600 as a trading
corporation. By the mid-eighteenth century it had its own army, was
fighting and winning wars against regional Indian rulers, and was
exercising de facto administrative control over large territories. After
the Battle of Plassey in 1757, it became the effective sovereign of
Bengal, with the power to tax, to administer justice, and to coerce the
labor of millions of people. The revenues of Bengal were used to purchase
Indian goods, which were sold in European and Asian markets, with the
profits returning to shareholders in London. This was not trade in any
meaningful sense. It was the conversion of sovereign power into private
profit. The East India Company operated in this way for nearly a century
before the 1857 uprising prompted the British Crown to take direct
control. The Company is the clearest historical case of a private
corporation granted, and exercising, governmental powers over subject
populations for the benefit of distant investors.

King Leopold II of Belgium's Congo Free State, established in 1885 and
operated until 1908, represents the most extreme documented case of
extraction rationalized as development and humanitarian improvement. The
Congo Free State was Leopold's personal property, not a Belgian colony.
He ran it through a system of concession companies that were entitled to
extract rubber and ivory from their territories and enforced production
quotas through systematic violence, including the mutilation of workers
and their family members who failed to meet quotas. The rubber boom of
the 1890s made the system especially brutal. The exposure of what was
happening, driven substantially by the investigative work of Edmund
Morel and the consul Roger Casement, produced one of the first
international human rights campaigns, and forced the transfer of the
Congo from Leopold's personal control to the Belgian state in 1908. The
Congo case established several features of subsequent human rights
discourse: the importance of documentation and testimony, the role of
metropolitan civil society in contesting abuses in colonial territories,
and the resistance of sovereign entities to external accountability even
when the evidence against them is overwhelming.

The United Fruit Company, which controlled banana production across much of Central America in the early twentieth century, illustrates a variant of corporate power that the East India Company case does not capture: the exercise of quasi-governmental authority in nominally independent countries rather than formal colonial territories. Guatemala, Honduras, and Costa Rica were sovereign states with their own governments and legal systems. United Fruit operated within them not through chartered authority or military conquest but through control of the infrastructure on which their export economies depended. The company owned the railroads that were the only means of moving cargo from the interior to the coast and controlled the ports through which that cargo moved. A government that wished to tax or regulate the company found that the commercial arteries of its economy were in the company's hands.

When Guatemala's elected president, Jacobo Árbenz, passed a land reform in 1952 that would have redistributed unused land including large holdings owned by United Fruit, the company lobbied the Eisenhower administration to characterize the reform as a communist threat. The CIA organized a coup in 1954 that removed Árbenz and installed a military government that reversed the land reform and restored the company's holdings. The operation was not conducted by a colonial government exercising sovereign authority. It was conducted by a corporation using political leverage to override a democratic decision made by an independent country about the use of its own land. The distinction between formal colonialism and this kind of corporate political interference is significant in form and limited in practical consequence for the people whose government was removed. Platform companies that have threatened to withdraw services from countries considering data-localization or interoperability requirements, or that have lobbied against privacy legislation in markets where they face no meaningful competition, are operating in the same register: using infrastructure dependency to constrain the choices available to nominally sovereign governments.

Colonial infrastructure followed a consistent logic regardless of which
European power built it. Railways in British India were built primarily
to move raw materials from the interior to the ports, not to connect
Indian cities to each other for the benefit of Indian commerce. Telegraph
lines connected colonial capitals to London, not villages to each other.
Ports were built at the points most convenient for loading export goods,
not where they would most efficiently serve local populations. The
infrastructure of colonialism was extraction infrastructure. This is not
an incidental feature of how it was built. It reflects who it was built
for and who paid for it. The territories that were colonized got
infrastructure that served the colonizer's interests, and got it in ways
that made them structurally dependent on the colonizing country's
transportation and communications networks.

The global cotton supply chain of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries
is the clearest historical example of the value capture problem that now
dominates debates about global technology supply chains. Sven Beckert's
account traces how cotton moved from slave-worked plantations in the
American South to mills in Lancashire to markets across the world, with
the financial and organizational power concentrated at the centers of
the system rather than at the points of production. The people who grew
the cotton captured the least value. The people who ginned, baled,
shipped, financed, and traded it captured more. The people who owned the
mills captured more still, and the merchants and financial intermediaries
who connected all of these functions captured value from all of them.
The same structure describes most current global supply chains, including
the supply chains for the devices and components that digital platforms
depend on.

A third form of extraction, less visible than raw materials or governance power, is the extraction of knowledge embedded in the communities from whom other resources are drawn. Colonial botanical gardens served this function systematically. The Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, operating from the mid-nineteenth century onward, sent collectors to colonized territories to gather plant specimens, seeds, and the agricultural knowledge associated with them. The communities that had developed this knowledge over generations received nothing and were not credited. The most consequential case is rubber. Henry Wickham collected seeds of Hevea brasiliensis from the Amazon in 1876 on behalf of the British government, brought them to Kew, and the resulting seedlings were shipped to British colonial territories in Malaya and Ceylon, where plantation industries were established at scale. By the early twentieth century, the Malayan plantation system had undercut the Brazilian rubber trade entirely. The value that Amazonian communities had built over generations was transferred to British colonial interests through the extraction of the biological and agricultural knowledge those communities held.

The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) and the Nagoya Protocol, which entered into force in 2014, represent the first systematic attempt to address this structure in law. The CBD established that genetic resources and the traditional knowledge associated with them belong to the communities from which they are drawn, and that access to those resources requires prior informed consent and the sharing of benefits derived from their use. These frameworks are imperfectly enforced and have significant gaps, but they establish a legal principle: the extraction of knowledge from a community without consent and without benefit-sharing is not a neutral transaction. That principle applies directly to the training of artificial intelligence systems on material scraped from the internet, text written by journalists and novelists, images produced by illustrators and photographers, code written by programmers. The people who produced this material receive nothing from the training process and then find themselves competing against systems built from their own accumulated labor. This is not copyright infringement in the conventional sense, which concerns the reproduction of specific works. It is bioprospecting, and the CBD and Nagoya Protocol provide a legal template for what a remedy might look like, including the requirement of prior informed consent, which no current AI training practice satisfies.

Platforms used by billions of people in Africa, South Asia, and Latin
America are owned almost entirely by companies headquartered in the
United States or China. The behavioral data generated by those users
is collected by the platform companies, processed in their data centers,
used to train their models, and monetized through their advertising
systems. The value generated by the attention and behavior of users in
Lagos or Dhaka flows to shareholders in California or Hangzhou. This is
resource extraction. The resource being extracted is not rubber or cotton
but attention and behavioral data. The extraction relationship is
structurally identical to colonial extraction: the periphery provides
the raw material, the center processes it, and the returns flow back to
the center.

The question of who governs speech on global platforms is a governance
question with a clear answer: American and Chinese companies, applying
American and Chinese legal frameworks and cultural assumptions, in
languages they support with varying degrees of completeness. Content
moderation at scale is conducted by large workforces of contractors,
mostly located in lower-wage countries, applying rules designed for
the English-language internet to content in hundreds of languages and
cultural contexts. The result is systematic under-moderation of harmful
content in lower-resource languages and over-moderation of content
in cultural contexts the rule designers did not understand. These are
not technical failures. They are the predictable outcomes of a system
in which the platforms that make content moderation decisions are not
accountable to the communities most affected by those decisions.

Infrastructure dependence was one of the primary mechanisms through
which formal decolonization failed to produce genuine economic
independence. Countries that had inherited railway gauges, port
facilities, and telegraph networks designed to serve colonial extraction
could not simply redesign them after independence without prohibitive
costs. The same structural dependence applies to digital infrastructure.
Countries whose populations conduct their communications, commerce, and
civic life on platforms owned by foreign corporations, whose cloud
computing runs on foreign-owned infrastructure, and whose software
ecosystems are controlled by foreign firms have a form of technological
dependence that is not qualitatively different from colonial infrastructure
dependence. Genuine technological self-determination requires owning the
stack, which requires capital, technical capacity, and political will
that most countries currently lack and that the dominant platform
companies have strong incentives to prevent them from developing.
