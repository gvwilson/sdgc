# Discarded

Topics that were considered but not included in the final lesson list:

-   *Avoiding Accountability*:
    Explores how limited liability and diffuse decision-making allow corporations to cause serious harm
    without any individual being held responsible.
    Uses the Ford Pinto, Enron, and the opioid crisis as case studies in structural insulation from consequence.

-   *The Commons*:
    A fragment arguing that public ownership and private markets are not the only options
    for governing shared resources,
    drawing on Elinor Ostrom's Nobel-Prize-winning research on sustainable community management of fisheries,
    forests, and irrigation systems.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Credit Card Network*:
    Interchange fees on card transactions are set unilaterally by networks,
    passed invisibly to consumers through higher prices,
    and transfer wealth from low-income cash users to high-income rewards cardholders---exactly
    as app store commissions and platform fees operate today.

-   *Professional Exclusion and How It Reproduces Itself*:
    Traces the consistent historical pattern by which high-status professions exclude women and minorities
    through formal barriers, informal gatekeeping, and "culture fit" criteria,
    using medicine, law, and computing as parallel examples.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Private Military Contractor*:
    Private military companies perform state functions---armed force, detention, intelligence---under
    weaker accountability frameworks than uniformed forces,
    mirroring how platforms govern speech and commerce at scale with no due process and no democratic oversight.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Patent Medicine Company*:
    Before the Pure Food and Drug Act, patent medicine companies made extravagant claims,
    buried harmful ingredients, and fought regulation using manufactured testimonials
    and doubt-seeding research---the same playbook used by tobacco, opioid manufacturers,
    and social media companies facing evidence of harm.

-   *Big Tech is Like the Auto Industry*:
    For six decades the industry attributed crash deaths to "the nut behind the wheel" rather than vehicle design,
    a deliberate framing that is the direct ancestor of "user error" as the preferred explanation
    for technology-related harm.

-   *Automation and Who Pays for Progress*:
    Every wave of labor-displacing technology concentrates immediate costs on workers
    whose skills are made redundant
    while distributing benefits broadly and with a delay,
    an asymmetry that is political rather than inevitable.

-   *Big Tech is Like the Chemical Industry*:
    Industrial pollution killed people visibly for decades before regulation arrived,
    with companies denying responsibility throughout,
    from Donora and the London Great Smog to Minamata Bay---a pattern that maps directly onto platform harms.

-   *The Children Who Went to School*:
    Mill owners in 1833 made arguments against child labor laws---family income dependency,
    freedom of contract, competitive harm---that reappear word-for-word in contemporary debates
    about gig worker classification and platform labor.

-   *How Organizations Mirror Their Structures*:
    Conway's Law---that organizations produce systems mirroring their communication structures---applies
    to hospitals, armies, and government agencies as much as to software teams,
    and means you cannot change the product without changing the organization.

-   *Why Discrimination Persists*:
    Becker's 1957 prediction that competitive markets would eliminate discrimination because
    it is costly to discriminate has been thoroughly falsified;
    discrimination persists because it often benefits the dominant group even when it is economically inefficient.

-   *Big Tech is Like the East India Company*:
    The structural features of colonialism---resource extraction, one-sided infrastructure, governance serving
    the metropole---have reappeared in how platforms operate in the Global South,
    setting rules, taking margins, and leaving risks to local populations.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Hollywood Studio*:
    The major studios of 1920--1948 owned production, controlled talent under exclusive contracts,
    ran distribution, and owned theaters---vertical integration that was broken up by antitrust action
    in 1948 and that platform bundling now reconstructs.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Nation-State (Without the Obligations)*:
    Platforms have populations larger than most countries, enforce their own legal systems via terms of service,
    and exercise quasi-sovereign functions over speech and commerce,
    but without the accountability obligations that democratic states carry.

-   *Big Tech is Like the NCAA*:
    The NCAA prohibits student athletes from being paid under the doctrine of amateurism
    while negotiating multi-billion-dollar television contracts on their behalf---a model of extracting
    labor value while denying worker status that maps cleanly onto platform gig arrangements.

-   *Big Tech is Like Planned Obsolescence*:
    Alfred Sloan's annual model change was a deliberate strategy to shorten replacement cycles;
    forced software upgrades, sunsetting APIs, and hardware lockout reproduce the same mechanism
    of manufactured obsolescence to drive recurring revenue.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Drug Company*:
    Pharmaceutical companies have suppressed adverse trial data, funded doubt-seeding research,
    and lobbied aggressively against regulation---a playbook the patent medicine industry invented
    a century earlier and that social media companies are now running.

-   *Big Tech is Like a Protection Racket*:
    A protection racket charges for protection against a threat the provider helps create or amplify,
    in a market where the customer cannot assess the threat independently
    or find an alternative supplier---matching how app store security fees, ad fraud detection,
    and brand-safety tools operate.

-   *Traditions*:
    Software developers are trained in mathematical, engineering, and scientific traditions,
    but almost never in the humanist tradition that asks why software is built by whom,
    for whom, and why certain problems and problem-solvers are treated as legitimate.
