# Big Tech is Like a Protection Racket

A [%g protection_racket "protection racket" %] is a business model in which
the provider creates or amplifies a threat, then charges for protection against it.
Three conditions make it viable.
First, the customer cannot independently assess the threat
or verify whether the protection being offered addresses a real risk.
Second, the provider holds a near-monopoly on the means of protection,
so the customer cannot shop for an alternative.
Third, the customer cannot protect herself through her own actions.
All three conditions allow the provider to set the price of protection
at whatever the customer will bear rather than at the cost of providing the service.
Remove any one condition and the racket becomes an ordinary market transaction.

The credit card payment network illustrates these conditions before we get to platforms.
When you pay with Visa or Mastercard,
the merchant pays an [%g interchange_fee "interchange fee" %] averaging roughly two percent of the transaction.
Merchants cannot refuse to accept the dominant cards without losing customers.
The fee is set by the network, not negotiated,
and is effectively a private tax on every commercial transaction.
The cost is passed to consumers through higher prices,
including those who pay cash and receive none of the benefits.
The network's "honor all cards" rule,
which required merchants to accept every card bearing the network logo
regardless of the interchange rate attached to that card,
foreclosed any merchant strategy for managing the cost.
A merchant who wanted to surcharge or decline premium cards,
which carried higher rates funding the rewards those cards offered,
risked losing network participation entirely.

The rewards system makes the regressivity of interchange concrete.
A frequent-flyer mile or a cashback percentage is funded partly through the interchange fee collected from the merchant.
High-income cardholders, who spend more and qualify for premium cards,
collect the most valuable rewards.
Low-income consumers, who are more likely to pay with cash or use basic debit cards,
pay the higher retail prices that merchants set to cover their interchange costs
and collect none of the benefits.
The system transfers money from the bottom of the income distribution toward the top,
invisibly, on every commercial transaction.

The Durbin Amendment, passed as part of the Dodd-Frank Act in 2010,
capped interchange fees on debit card transactions
and required networks to allow merchants to route transactions through alternative networks.
The financial industry contested it immediately and has continued to seek its modification or repeal.
As a model of regulation it demonstrates both that interchange can be constrained by law
and that the industry will fight any such constraint aggressively.

App store fees, marketplace transaction fees, and payment processing fees operate by the same logic:
unavoidable for any business that wants access to the customer base,
set unilaterally by the network operator,
and passed through to end users who have no visibility into it.
The invisibility of these fees is not accidental.
Platforms and networks have a structural interest in keeping transaction costs invisible to end users.
A consumer who understood that fifteen percent of every purchase made through an app store
went to the platform operator,
or that two percent of every card transaction went to the card network and issuing bank,
might draw different conclusions about the pricing and market power of these systems.
The fee is buried in the retail price,
set by the merchant or developer who has already absorbed the cost.

Apple describes its App Store review process as protecting users from unsafe apps
and characterizes mandatory in-app payment processing as necessary to ensure secure transactions.
These claims are not entirely false.
Malware is a real threat, and Apple's review does catch some of it.
But the fee for this safety service happens to be thirty percent of app revenue,
which is not the cost of security review.
When safety language is applied to rules that primarily generate revenue,
it functions to foreclose criticism
by associating resistance to the fee with indifference to user harm.
This is precisely how protection racket logic operates in legitimate organizational contexts.

Switching costs are the mechanism that keeps customers in a protection arrangement
after they have concluded the fee is too high.
A developer who has spent years building an iOS app,
acquiring users,
accumulating App Store reviews,
and integrating Apple's APIs
faces a complete loss of that investment if they leave the platform.
This is not the same as leaving a subscription service.
It is closer to abandoning a business.
The switching cost is high enough that developers will accept fee structures they would reject if starting fresh,
because the cost of the alternative is greater than the cost of continued participation.
This dynamic does not require coercion in any narrow sense.
The costs that make exit unacceptable were created by the developer's own investments
within the platform's system.

Diego Gambetta's analysis of the Sicilian Mafia as an economic institution
turns on the observation that the Mafia produces and sells a commodity:
private enforcement of agreements in an environment where legal enforcement is unavailable or unreliable.
Gambetta's point is that this is not different in kind from what legitimate institutions do.
It differs in the legal status of the provider
and in whether the threat environment was manufactured or merely exploited.
The insight applies to platforms:
where legal mechanisms for resolving disputes between app developers and Apple are inadequate or unavailable,
Apple functions as the private enforcement mechanism, at a price,
for access to a transaction environment it controls.

Charles Tilly's argument about early modern European state formation extends this logic to political institutions.
Tilly observed that the process by which European states consolidated
was structurally indistinguishable from organized crime:
monopolizing violence within a territory,
extracting revenue in exchange for protection from external threats,
and suppressing competing protection providers.
The states that survived were the ones that successfully made their protection monopoly legitimate,
which they did primarily by making the alternative to their rule visibly worse.
The distinction between a legitimate government and a protection racket is,
in Tilly's account,
historical and contingent rather than categorical.
This framing makes visible the stakes of platform regulation:
whether the platform's claim to govern a market and extract revenue from it
becomes legitimate through law,
or remains contested as a form of private extraction.

The question is not whether platforms provide genuine safety services,
because they do.
The question is whether the fee they charge for those services
is set by the cost of providing them,
by competitive market pressure,
or by the platform's monopoly power over the distribution channel.
Where the answer is the third option,
the structure is that of a protection racket
regardless of whether the protection provided is real.
