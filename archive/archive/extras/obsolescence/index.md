# Big Tech is Like Planned Obsolescence

In the 1920s, General Motors president Alfred Sloan introduced the
annual model change as a deliberate commercial strategy. Each year's
Chevrolet, Buick, or Cadillac was styled to make the previous year's
model look dated, even when mechanical differences were minimal. The
aim was to shorten the replacement cycle and increase sales. Sloan
called it [%g dynamic_obsolescence "dynamic obsolescence" %]. By 1960, Vance Packard documented
how the principle had spread from automobiles to appliances to
clothing, creating a consumer economy premised on designed
dissatisfaction with products that still worked.

The software equivalent is forced version deprecation: support ends
for operating systems and applications that remain functional,
hardware slows through OS updates designed for newer processors, and
APIs change in ways that break existing integrations. The mechanism is
technical rather than aesthetic, but the structural result is the
same: the user's existing investment loses value on a schedule
determined by the seller, not the user.

Alfred Sloan's insight at General Motors was that the automobile market was approaching saturation. Most American households that could afford a car already had one, and those cars still worked. The logical response to a saturated market, from the consumer's perspective, is to keep using what works. Sloan's response was to make "what works" feel inadequate through the annual model change. Each year's cars were restyled to make the previous year's look dated, not through mechanical improvement but through aesthetic manipulation. Chrome, tailfins, and color schemes changed on a schedule, ensuring that a car purchased in 1955 looked visibly old by 1957. The strategy replaced genuine innovation with designed dissatisfaction and built that dissatisfaction into the production calendar.

The Phoebus cartel of 1925 is a more direct case. General Electric, Philips, Osram, and other major light bulb manufacturers formed a cartel and agreed to limit the rated life of incandescent bulbs to 1,000 hours. Bulbs that lasted longer already existed; manufacturers were producing them. The cartel required members to pay fines if their bulbs lasted beyond the agreed limit. The purpose was not to protect consumers from unreliable products but to ensure that consumers needed to replace bulbs frequently enough to sustain sales volume. The Phoebus cartel is among the most thoroughly documented cases of deliberately engineered obsolescence, and it operated for over a decade before the Second World War disrupted it.

Vance Packard's *The Waste Makers* (1960) documented the extension of the same logic from automobiles and light bulbs to the entire consumer economy. Packard distinguished between [%g psychological_obsolescence "psychological obsolescence" %], in which a product is made to feel dated while remaining functional, and [%g functional_obsolescence "functional obsolescence" %], in which a product is made to fail sooner than its materials and engineering require. Both were deliberate strategies, both were visible across major consumer product categories, and both depended on consumers being unable to distinguish designed failure from natural wear. Packard's argument was not that corporations were unusually wicked but that the replacement-cycle logic was a structural feature of an economy organized around consumption growth.

Software deprecation operates through the same mechanism with different implementation. When a software vendor announces end-of-life for an operating system or application, it does not mean the software has stopped working. It means the vendor will stop issuing security patches. This creates a genuine risk for users who remain on the deprecated system, but the risk is manufactured: the vendor could continue patching but chooses not to, because continued support for old versions reduces the incentive to upgrade. Hardware vendors compound this by releasing operating system updates that perform poorly on older processors, using software to make functioning hardware feel slow. The user's rational response is to replace hardware that still works. The mechanism is technical rather than aesthetic, but the commercial logic is Sloan's.

The [%g right_to_repair "right-to-repair" %] movement is a contemporary response to this structure that mirrors early consumer advocacy with considerable precision. Repair advocates argue that buyers of a product have the right to maintain, modify, and repair it using third-party parts and service providers, and that manufacturers should not be able to use software locks, proprietary fasteners, or restricted parts supply to force users back to the manufacturer's service network. Apple's opposition to right-to-repair legislation, John Deere's use of software to prevent farmers from repairing their own tractors, and medical device manufacturers' restrictions on hospital biomedical engineers are all applications of the same strategy: control the post-sale relationship by controlling the conditions under which the product can continue to function.

The cost distribution of the replacement cycle is asymmetric in a consistent direction. Manufacturers receive revenue from each replacement sale. The user bears the cost of the replacement purchase and the cost of discarding a functional product. The environmental cost of electronic waste, which includes heavy metals, rare earth elements, and manufacturing emissions, falls on neither the manufacturer nor the user in any direct accounting sense but on the communities and ecosystems where waste is processed. This externalization is not accidental. Regulatory frameworks that would require manufacturers to bear end-of-life costs would change the incentive to shorten product life; the absence of such frameworks is why [%g planned_obsolescence "planned obsolescence" %] remains commercially rational.
