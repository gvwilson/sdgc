# Big Tech is Like the Chemical Industry

Industrial pollution killed people visibly and in large numbers for
decades before it was regulated. The Donora smog disaster of 1948
killed twenty people in a Pennsylvania mill town and made thousands
ill. The London Great Smog of 1952 killed an estimated four thousand
people in five days. In Japan, the Chisso Corporation discharged
mercury into Minamata Bay from the 1930s onward; the resulting disease
killed and disabled thousands, and the company denied responsibility
for decades. The Cuyahoga River in Cleveland caught fire; the Rhine
was effectively dead by the 1970s from chemical runoff; the Seveso
disaster released dioxin across a swath of northern Italy
in 1976. These were not isolated events. They were the predictable
outcome of an industrial system that had no obligation to account for
the costs it imposed on air, water, and the people who depended on
them.

The tech industry's harms follow the same pattern: documented, denied,
attributed to individual bad actors, and addressed only when political
pressure made continued inaction untenable. The playbook the chemical
industry used to resist environmental regulation is the playbook
platforms have used to resist content and privacy regulation.

Before the regulatory frameworks of the 1960s and 1970s existed, the
harms of industrial pollution were not unknown. They were documented,
understood by scientists working inside and outside the affected
industries, and largely ignored by the entities responsible for producing
them. Industrial hygienists had linked benzene exposure to leukemia by
the 1920s. The health effects of lead in paint and gasoline were
documented in the public health literature decades before lead was removed
from either product. Asbestos manufacturers had internal research
establishing the causal link to mesothelioma while simultaneously
funding public relations campaigns disputing that link. The problem was
not a lack of information. It was a legal and regulatory structure that
placed the burden of proof on the harmed rather than the polluter, and a
political structure in which the polluting industries had substantially
more influence over regulatory outcomes than the communities absorbing
the pollution.

Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring*, published in 1962, is the central text
in the history of environmental regulation not because it introduced
novel science but because it made the science legible to a mass audience
and connected it to a political argument. Carson documented the effects
of pesticides, particularly DDT, on bird populations and food chains,
and argued that chemical industry claims of safety were based on
inadequate testing and that the regulatory agencies responsible for
oversight had been too closely aligned with the industries they were
supposed to regulate. The chemical industry's response was immediate and
systematic: it hired scientists to attack her methods, questioned her
credentials, and attempted to prevent publication. That response,
described in contemporaneous documents and subsequent histories, is the
template that industries have followed in contesting inconvenient
research ever since. The tactics deployed against Carson were deployed
again against the scientists documenting the harms of tobacco, leaded
gasoline, and platform algorithms.

The Chisso Corporation's discharge of methylmercury into Minamata Bay in
Japan represents the most extensively documented case of corporate
concealment of industrial harm. Chisso was the primary employer in
Minamata. The mercury-contaminated wastewater it discharged from the
1930s onward bioaccumulated in fish and shellfish, which the local
fishing community ate as a dietary staple. The resulting neurological
disease, which became known as Minamata disease, caused severe disability
and death among thousands of people. Chisso's internal researchers
identified the company's wastewater as the cause in 1959. The company
suppressed that finding, continued discharging mercury, altered its
outflow location to disperse contamination more widely, and blocked
access to the bay to prevent independent scientific sampling. The full
legal reckoning, when it came decades later, was partial: victims received
compensation, but the legal findings did not fully acknowledge the extent
of the deliberate concealment.

The United States Environmental Protection Agency and the United
Kingdom's Clean Air Act of 1956 represent different political paths to
similar regulatory outcomes. The UK Act followed directly from the London
Great Smog, a disaster visible and lethal enough that political inaction
became untenable within months. The US EPA was established in 1970 as the
outcome of a longer process of political organizing that combined
scientific documentation, public advocacy, litigation, and legislative
pressure over roughly a decade. The paths differed in their triggers and
their institutional designs, but both produced regulatory agencies with
authority to set pollution standards and enforce compliance, and both
achieved measurable reductions in the pollutants they targeted.

[%g transboundary_pollution "Transboundary pollution" %] created a regulatory problem that national
legislation could not address on its own. The Rhine, which passes through
Switzerland, France, Germany, and the Netherlands before reaching the
North Sea, was effectively dead across much of its length by the 1970s
from industrial runoff, primarily from chemical plants in the upper
river. A polluting factory in Basel imposed costs on communities in the
Netherlands, but no mechanism connected the two. The Rhine Action
Programme, initiated in 1987 and substantially driven by the 1986
Sandoz chemical spill that killed fish across 400 kilometers of river,
established a framework for coordinated regulation across national
borders. It is an early and relatively successful example of the
governance problem that is now central to the regulation of digital
platforms: how to regulate entities whose operations cross jurisdictions
and whose harms are distributed across countries that have different
regulatory frameworks and different political relationships with the
regulated industry.

Regulatory capture is the process by which regulated industries gradually
gain sufficient influence over their regulatory agencies to determine,
or substantially constrain, the agencies' behavior. It operates through
several mechanisms: the revolving door between industry and agency
employment, through which regulators who expect to return to industry
employment have incentives to maintain good relations with the regulated
firms; the lobbying and campaign finance systems that give industries
political influence over the legislative bodies that fund and oversee
the agencies; the strategic placement of industry-aligned appointees in
leadership positions; and the defunding of agencies to the point where
they lack the technical staff to conduct independent analysis. All of
these mechanisms were documented in the environmental regulatory context.
All of them appear again in the history of pharmaceutical and financial
regulation, and all of them are currently active in the context of
platform regulation.

Environmental regulation achieved significant and measurable results
in the domains it directly targeted. Levels of the major air pollutants
regulated under US law are substantially lower than they were in 1970.
The Rhine supports salmon again. Minamata Bay has been formally certified
as decontaminated, though that certification is disputed by some
survivors and scientists. What was not achieved is at least as important
as what was. Regulation addressed the visible, acute harms more
effectively than the chronic, dispersed ones. It addressed pollutants
that had already been identified and that had politically mobilized
affected communities more effectively than novel pollutants whose harms
were uncertain or whose victims were not organized. And it did not
address the underlying incentive structure that makes pollution
profitable: it raised the cost of some forms of pollution without
eliminating the basic dynamic in which firms can profit by imposing costs
on others that the firms do not have to bear themselves.
