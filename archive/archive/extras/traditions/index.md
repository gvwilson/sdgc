# Traditions

Most programmers are trained in one or more of three traditions: the
mathematical (programs are abstract objects that are correct or
incorrect), the engineering (programs affect the world and should be
reliable), and the scientific (programs are models of information
processes). A fourth tradition, the humanist tradition, asks why
software is built the way it is by the people who build it, and which
problems and problem-solvers are considered legitimate. This tradition
is almost never taught in computing programs, which is itself a
political choice. Its tools are drawn from sociology, history, and
political science, and are the subject of most of the lessons that
follow.

Each of the three standard traditions has a characteristic set of assumptions. The mathematical tradition treats programs as formal objects whose correctness can be proven independent of who wrote them or why. The engineering tradition treats programs as artifacts that must be reliable under real-world conditions, and holds engineers responsible for failures that cause harm. The scientific tradition treats programs as models of computational processes, to be validated against observed behavior. All three traditions share an implicit assumption: that the interesting questions about software are technical questions, and that the social context of software production is background noise rather than a constitutive factor in what gets built and how.

The humanist tradition rejects that assumption. It treats programs as products of human labor, produced within specific social arrangements, by people who carry assumptions about whose problems are worth solving and whose skills count as expertise. Under this framing, a decision to build one kind of system rather than another is always also a social decision, shaped by the distribution of power and resources among the people making it. The question "how does this work?" is separate from the question "why does this exist, and who benefits from it?" The humanist tradition asks the second question, and takes it seriously.

Once you adopt this framing, questions that were previously invisible become tractable. Why have search engines indexed some kinds of content far more thoroughly than others? Why does facial recognition software perform worse on darker-skinned faces? Why did early voice recognition systems fail to recognize women's voices reliably? These are not engineering puzzles with purely technical solutions. They are consequences of choices made by specific groups of people working in specific institutional contexts with specific resources and blind spots. The humanist tradition provides tools for analyzing those choices systematically rather than treating them as accidents.

The resistance of computer science departments to this tradition is itself worth examining. It is not simply that the tools are unfamiliar, though they are. It is that adopting the humanist framing forces a reckoning with the fact that technical decisions are political decisions, and that the discipline's self-presentation as value-neutral is a position, not a description. Departments that have built their prestige on the mathematical and engineering traditions have institutional reasons to treat the humanist tradition as outside their scope. That exclusion is a choice, and it has consequences for what kinds of researchers get trained, what kinds of problems get funded, and what kinds of harm go unexamined.

This course is organized around tools drawn from outside the standard computing curriculum: history, sociology, [%g political_economy "political economy" %], and institutional analysis. These tools do not replace technical knowledge. They make it possible to ask questions that technical knowledge alone cannot answer.
