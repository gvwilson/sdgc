# Big Tech is Like the NCAA

The National Collegiate Athletic Association controls who can
participate in college sport, sets the rules of competition, and
negotiates multi-billion-dollar television contracts on behalf of
member institutions. The people whose labor generates this revenue,
the student athletes, are prohibited from receiving compensation above
the cost of attendance under the doctrine of [%g amateurism "amateurism" %]: the claim
that payment would corrupt the educational character of college sport
and undermine competitive fairness. This ideology was invented in the
late 19th century by British aristocrats who wanted to exclude
working-class athletes from competition and has been maintained in the
US primarily because it is profitable for the institutions that
enforce it.

In NCAA v. Alston (2021), the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that the
NCAA's compensation restrictions violated antitrust law. Justice
Kavanaugh wrote in a concurrence that "the NCAA's business model would
be flatly illegal in almost any other industry in America." Platforms
that prohibit creators from monetizing their audiences off-platform,
or that extract a percentage of all revenue without compensating for
the content that generates it, are operating the same model.

Amateurism as a formal doctrine was invented by British sporting clubs in the late 19th century, and its original purpose had nothing to do with educational values. It was a mechanism for excluding working-class athletes from competition with upper-class ones. A working-class rower who had ever been paid for physical labor could be disqualified under amateur rules even if he had never earned money from sport. The doctrine traveled to American universities and was there repurposed: instead of excluding workers from competition, it was used to prevent workers from being compensated. The ideology shifted from class purity to educational purity, but the economic function remained identical. Labor was extracted; compensation was withheld; the rationale was philosophical.

The revenue structure of college athletics makes clear where the money goes. Major football and basketball programs generate hundreds of millions of dollars annually through broadcasting rights, licensing agreements, ticket sales, and merchandise. This money flows to universities, athletic departments, coaches, conference offices, and the NCAA itself. Coaching salaries at major programs regularly exceed five million dollars per year. The athletes whose performance generates this revenue receive scholarships that cover tuition, room, and board, and they are prohibited from receiving more. The gap between what the labor produces and what the laborer receives is among the largest in any industry operating inside the United States.

The NCAA defended this arrangement for decades by arguing that athletic scholarships were adequate and appropriate compensation because they provided educational opportunity. This argument has a precise structural parallel in how platforms describe their relationship to creators and users. Platforms routinely argue that access to the platform's audience, tools, and distribution infrastructure constitutes sufficient return for the content and data that users provide. The scholarship argument and the exposure argument are the same argument: the organization that controls access to the market claims that access itself is compensation, and uses that claim to justify not paying for the labor that makes the market valuable.

The Supreme Court's ruling in NCAA v. Alston (2021) disposed of this argument in antitrust terms. The Court held unanimously that the NCAA's restrictions on education-related benefits violated the Sherman Act. Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence went further than the majority opinion required, stating directly that the NCAA was asking the Court to sanction an arrangement that would be illegal in any other industry. The ruling did not immediately require payment of athletes, but it removed the legal foundation on which the compensation restrictions had rested and made further challenges substantially easier to bring.

The [%g nil_rights "Name, Image, and Likeness" %] change that followed Alston illustrates how much value had been suppressed. Within months of the NCAA's capitulation on NIL rights in 2021, athletes in major programs were signing endorsement deals worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. Some signed deals worth millions. The NCAA had resisted NIL rights for decades on the grounds that allowing athletes to profit from their own identities would undermine amateurism. What the rapid emergence of the NIL market actually demonstrated was that the labor had always been worth far more than the scholarship. The decades of resistance had not been about protecting educational values; it had been about capturing that value for the institutions rather than the athletes.

Platform terms of service replicate the NCAA structure in several respects. Exclusive licensing clauses require creators to grant platforms broad rights over content as a condition of access. Off-platform monetization restrictions prevent creators from directing their audiences to other revenue streams. Revenue-sharing arrangements are set unilaterally by the platform and change without negotiation. The underlying claim, in each case, is that the platform's provision of audience access and distribution infrastructure justifies whatever terms it chooses to impose. This is the scholarship argument. The platform owns the market; access to the market is your compensation; you are not entitled to negotiate.
