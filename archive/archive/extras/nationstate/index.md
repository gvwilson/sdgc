# Big Tech is Like a Nation-State (Without the Obligations)

Major platforms have populations larger than most countries:
two billion daily users exceeds the population of every country except China and India.
They have territories that exist in but are not fully subject to national jurisdiction.
They have legal systems:
terms of service that govern conduct and are enforced through moderation and suspension.
They exercise what scholars of international law call
[%g quasi_sovereign "quasi-sovereign" %] functions,
deciding who may speak, who may transact,
and who may participate in economic and social life
at a scale that few governments can match.

What platforms do not have are the obligations that attach to sovereignty.
A government that surveilled its population at the scale platforms do,
made binding decisions about speech without due process,
and extracted a percentage of all commercial transactions without a legislative mandate
would be in violation of the constitutional frameworks of every liberal democracy.
Platforms do all of this and characterize it as a terms of service update.

Private military and security companies are a useful comparison.
They perform functions historically understood as the exclusive domain of the state,
including armed force, detention, and intelligence collection,
under significantly weaker accountability frameworks than uniformed forces.
Military justice does not cover them.
Civilian criminal law has struggled to reach their conduct overseas.
Their accountability runs to the contracting client rather than to the public.
The Nisour Square massacre in Baghdad in 2007,
in which Blackwater contractors killed seventeen civilians,
went through more than a decade of legal proceedings without definitive resolution:
federal charges, a conviction, an appeal, a remand, a presidential pardon.
Whether what happened constituted a war crime or a negligent shooting
remained legally unresolved for years.
The civilians were killed by private actors operating under a contract,
in a country where those private actors were legally immune under agreements negotiated by their client.
This is what a legal gray zone looks like when it produces state-level harm.

The concept of quasi-sovereignty in international law was developed to describe entities
that exercise governmental functions without formal state status:
colonial trading companies, international organizations, and similar bodies.
Platforms fit this description more closely than the entities that originally generated it.
They have populations, rules, enforcement mechanisms,
and effective control over the conditions of participation in economic and social life.
What they lack are the obligations that international and constitutional law attach to sovereignty:
no requirement to respect rights,
no obligation to provide due process,
no democratic mandate.

Terms of service are private law in the functional sense.
The platform writes them unilaterally,
changes them without meaningful notice,
and enforces them through systems that are not required to be consistent, transparent, or proportionate.
A user who violates terms of service may lose access to their account,
their stored data, their audience, or their income.
The process by which that determination is made is internal to the company,
not subject to external review,
and not required to provide the user an opportunity to be heard before the decision is executed.

The due process gap between platforms and governments is structural, not incidental.
In the United States, the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments require notice,
an opportunity to be heard,
a decision by a neutral party,
and the ability to appeal.
A government agency that terminated someone's benefits or revoked their license
would be required to provide these protections.
A platform that terminates an account is not.
Account holders have fewer procedural rights against a platform that terminates their income
than a citizen has against a government agency that delays their tax refund.

Content moderation is the equivalent function for speech.
Platforms govern billions of people's expressive activity:
they write the rules,
enforce them through moderation systems that are largely automated,
and provide appeals processes that are internal, slow, and not legally required to produce consistent results.
The Facebook Oversight Board represents one attempt to create something resembling independent review,
but it cannot compel Facebook to change its policies,
it handles a tiny fraction of moderation decisions,
and it has no enforcement mechanism.
No external authority can require a platform to restore an account,
explain a removal decision,
or apply its stated rules consistently.

Payment processors exercise a parallel form of private governance over commercial activity.
Visa, Mastercard, and PayPal have terminated relationships with legal businesses,
including firearms retailers, adult content platforms, and political organizations,
without due process, without explanation,
and without any legal requirement to provide either.
A business that loses access to card payments loses access
to the commercial infrastructure of the modern economy.
The payment processor's decision is functionally a license revocation,
imposed by a private company with no democratic accountability for the decision.

Cross-border operation is central to how both private military contractors and technology platforms
minimize accountability.
A contractor operating in Iraq is not subject to Iraqi criminal law,
is not subject to US military justice,
and faces long odds in US federal courts due to evidentiary problems and jurisdictional disputes.
Platforms similarly route operations, data, and revenue through multiple jurisdictions
to avoid the regulatory frameworks of any single one.
The legal and regulatory gaps between jurisdictions are not accidents.
They are resources that well-resourced private actors exploit deliberately.

[%g section_230 "Section 230" %] of the Communications Decency Act, enacted in 1996,
provides that platforms cannot be treated as publishers or speakers
for content created by third parties.
This immunity was designed to allow early internet services to moderate content
without incurring liability for content they failed to remove.
It was granted without corresponding obligations:
platforms received immunity from publisher liability
without being required to provide the procedural protections or content standards
that would apply to a regulated publisher.
The result is a legal framework in which platforms have the immunities of conduits
and the editorial power of publishers,
without the obligations of either.

Democratic accountability for these functions would require, at minimum,
that the rules governing them be made by elected bodies,
that the people affected have some mechanism for challenging decisions,
and that violations have consequences proportionate to the harm.
None of these conditions currently apply
to private military contractors in the field
or to platform governance of speech and commerce.
The political economy of the resistance is straightforward:
the companies performing these functions have strong financial interests
in the existing accountability vacuum,
and those who benefit from contracting out state functions
have interests in preserving the arrangement.
