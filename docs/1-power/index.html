2  Power and Institutions

Why do some people’s preferences become policy and other people’s don’t?

NoteLearning Objectives
  • Describe the three faces of power (decision, agenda, preference-shaping).
  • Define an institution as “the rules of the game” (formal and informal).
  • Explain why concentrated interests usually defeat diffuse ones.

The political scientist Harold Lasswell defined politics in 1936 as the study of “who gets what, when, how.” The definition carries no assumption that the distribution is fair, no requirement that the process be democratic, and no claim that what gets distributed is material. It is simply a description of what happens whenever a group of people who do not fully agree on goals or values still have to act together.

Think of a product decision at your company: an API change, a layoff, a content-moderation rule, a shift in the roadmap. Who actually decided it? Who set the agenda so that this was the decision on the table, rather than some other one? And whose interests won? Those three questions are the spine of this chapter. Power is not just about who wins a visible fight; it is about who decides what is worth fighting about in the first place, and who shapes what people think they want.

2.1 The Three Faces of Power

The most intuitive account of power is the one Steven Lukes calls the first face: who wins when there is a visible decision (Lukes 2021). When a board votes, a legislature passes a bill, or a manager picks one proposal over another, the winner is exercising the most obvious kind of power, and it is easy to see because it happens in public.

The second face is harder to see, because it happens before the vote. It is the power to decide what gets onto the agenda in the first place. A roadmap that never includes privacy work, an API that never exposes a particular capability, a meeting whose agenda is fixed before the people most affected walk into the room: these are all exercises of the second face. The people who control what is even considered never have to win the argument, because the argument never happens. This is why a “neutral” default or a “technical” constraint is very often an act of agenda-setting dressed in the language of inevitability.

The third face is the deepest. It is the power to shape what people even want, so that some options never occur to anyone. The third face is at work when an arrangement feels natural, obvious, or inevitable rather than chosen, and its catchphrase is “we don’t do politics here.” The sentence almost always means something more precise: the winners already have what they want, and they can afford to describe their arrangement as simply how things are. In 2021, the software company Basecamp banned “societal and political discussions” at work, and roughly a third of its staff resigned. The decision to ban discussion of politics was itself a political decision, made unilaterally by the winning coalition. “We don’t do politics here” is not the absence of politics; it is the third face doing its quiet work.

2.2 Institutions Are the Rules of the Game

Douglass North defined an institution as “the rules of the game”: the formal rules such as laws and contracts, and the informal constraints such as norms, conventions, and taboos, that structure human interaction (North 1990). The definition is deliberately broad, because institutions are everywhere, and they do more than constrain. They allocate advantage. A rule is never neutral, because every rule makes some outcomes easier and others harder, and someone benefits from each one.

The English enclosure movement is the cleanest illustration. Between 1750 and 1850, Parliament passed hundreds of private Enclosure Acts, each one extinguishing common rights over a specific area and converting that land into private property. The process was not neutral arbitration between competing claims: the landowners who stood to benefit were the same class that controlled Parliament, while the commoners whose rights were extinguished had no equivalent political representation (Bollier 2025). The rules changed, and the change had a direction. Institutions are the place where that direction gets set.

2.3 Design Decisions Are Governance Decisions

A piece of software is an institution. Default settings, permission models, and rate limits are rules that allocate advantage just as surely as a zoning ordinance or a tax code, which means a design decision is a governance decision, whether or not anyone in the room is willing to say so.

The 1886 American railroad gauge change makes the point concrete. Southern railroads ran on a five-foot gauge while northern lines ran on the standard four feet eight and a half inches, so freight crossing the boundary had to be unloaded and reloaded. Southern carriers profited from that break: the “incompatibility” was a market weapon, not a technical accident, and the “technical” choice was political all the way down. The eventual conversion to a single gauge was a decision about who would capture the value of moving goods. It was also an instance of path dependence: the gauge had been chosen for reasons that no longer applied, but once the tracks, the rolling stock, and the jobs were built around it, structural lock-in made changing course mean tearing up an entire landscape.

Platforms reproduce the same logic at a finer grain. The medieval Church provided civil infrastructure— records, courts, hospitals, schools— and collected a tithe of roughly one-tenth of productive output in return, an obligation enforced whether or not you were a believer. The App Store’s thirty percent commission is a tithe on productive activity conducted within its jurisdiction. Excommunication—removal from the Church’s services— meant you could no longer conduct legal business; deplatforming does the same to a creator or seller who loses access to customers, tools, and markets with no external authority to appeal to (Southern 1990).

Institutions also produce behavior. When Wells Fargo fired 5,300 employees over fake accounts—“eight is great”— the wrongdoing did not flow from five thousand bad individuals. It flowed from impossible quotas, aggressive measurement, and a reward structure that made the bad behavior the rational response. The incentives produced the wrongdoing, and the institution shaped the incentives.

Institutions can also be self-fulfilling. Terms of service written for adversaries, and moderation systems that treat everyone as a suspect, produce the distrust they assume. The contrast is Elinor Ostrom’s work on the commons: communities that governed shared resources sustainably for centuries, not through privatization or state control, but through locally developed rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions (Ostrom 2015). Design a system as if people are untrustworthy, and you get untrustworthy behavior; design it as if they can govern themselves, and you sometimes get self-governance.

The same point applies to the corporate form itself. The usual excuse for why tech companies are not worker cooperatives or other equitable structures is that cooperative governance is too slow and cooperative financing too limited. That explanation is conveniently incomplete: it omits that the founders, lawyers, and venture capitalists making the choice benefit most from the conventional structure (Whyte and Whyte 1991; Kelly 2012). The “neutral” form is a choice with distributional consequences.

2.4 Platforms as Quasi-Governments

When a seller on eBay disputes a transaction, eBay adjudicates the claim. When a developer’s app is removed from the App Store, Apple’s internal review process is the only available appeal. When Facebook removes content in a country with regulated speech, it is making regulatory decisions in a jurisdiction where it has been granted no regulatory authority. Platforms are quasi-governments, and the word “quasi” is doing a lot of work.

The clearest test of what governance actually is came from an unlikely source. In the days after the March 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, the yakuza—Japan’s organized crime syndicates— supplied food, water, and emergency goods to affected communities faster than official relief channels could mobilize (Kaplan and Dubro 2012). The lesson is not that organized crime is admirable; it is that governance is whoever can actually get things done. When formal institutions are slow, whoever controls logistics, relationships, and trust governs in their place.

That is the position platforms are building for themselves. George Pullman built a company town in Illinois in the 1880s, where the company owned the workers’ housing, stores, church, library, and bank. When Pullman cut wages during the depression of 1893 while holding rents fixed, workers could not cushion the blow by cutting other expenses, because the company controlled those too. They had no recourse and nothing left to lose, and the 1894 Pullman Strike paralyzed rail traffic across the country and required federal troops to suppress— which tells you something about what total control eventually produces (Lindsey 1942).

The difference between an employer and an infrastructure provider is a matter of degree, not kind. An employer controls your income; an infrastructure provider controls the conditions under which you can earn, spend, and participate in social life. Mao-era China’s danwei work units controlled employment, housing, healthcare, food rations, and education through a single institution, so that to be removed from a danwei was to lose all of them at once. A super-app like WeChat integrates messaging, payments, healthcare bookings, government services, and social identity into a single system; a user whose account is suspended loses not just a communication tool but the infrastructure through which they conduct their civic and commercial life (Stoller 2019; Walder 2017). Foxconn’s Shenzhen campuses, which house hundreds of thousands of workers in company dormitories with company stores and company transportation, are the Pullman model at a scale Pullman could not have imagined.

2.5 Legibility and Institutional Money

James C. Scott argued that states standardize people so they can be taxed, drafted, and policed. Surnames, house numbers, passports, and identity registers exist not because the state wants to know you, but because it wants to be able to act upon you (Scott 1998). Scott called this legibility: the degree to which an institution can observe, measure, and administer a population through standardized categories. The technical history of identification is also a political history of control, and South Africa’s apartheid-era pass system— which became the world’s first large-scale biometric register by 1986— is the sharpest reminder of it (Breckenridge 2016).

The private sector has its own version of the same impulse. Philanthrocapitalism—the use of private foundations and large-scale giving to shape public policy without democratic accountability— converts taxable wealth into permanently controlled endowments. Donor-advised funds, which held over $230 billion in assets by 2022, let a donor take an immediate tax deduction while retaining the ability to direct distributions for decades. The Giving Pledge, launched in 2010, invites billionaires to promise publicly that they will give away most of their wealth— a promise that is not legally binding and is often fulfilled by moving assets into funds and foundations that remain under the donor’s control (Giridharadas 2018). For many years the Gates Foundation was the second-largest funder of the World Health Organization, behind only the United States government. Whatever the intentions, that is influence exercised without the legitimacy that comes from being elected or removable.

2.6 Concentrated Interests, Diffuse Costs

Mancur Olson explained why some groups reliably win and others reliably lose. It is easier to organize a small group with a large stake than a large group with a small stake (Olson 1965). Ad-tech firms—few, organized, and rich— can coordinate, hire lobbyists, and file comments on proposed regulations. Individual users—many, unorganized, and each with a small stake— mostly cannot. This single asymmetry predicts much of tech policy: producers beat consumers and platforms beat users, not because anyone is smarter or more virtuous, but because the costs of organizing are distributed unevenly.

Selectorate theory sharpens the point. Leaders of countries, companies, and volunteer organizations need enough support to stay in power, and they get it by distributing benefits to a minimum necessary winning coalition rather than to the whole population (Mesquita and Smith 2011). Everyone else is interchangeable, whatever the language about family. In November 2022, after laying off about a third of its workforce, Elon Musk emailed the remaining Twitter employees asking them to click a button confirming that they were committed to working “hardcore,” with those who did not click treated as having resigned; a few months earlier, Twitter’s leadership had described the company as a family. The winning coalition is smaller than the family, and it is the coalition that gets served first.

None of this is a secret to the people who succeed at it. Jeffrey Pfeffer found that political skill predicts advancement better than technical competence, and that managers who ignore politics systematically lose to those who do not (Pfeffer 1992). That is an uncomfortable finding for an industry that likes to imagine decisions are made by the best argument. They are made by the people who can assemble a coalition, set an agenda, and make their own interests look natural. The question is never whether power is present; it is whose interests it is serving.

NoteTakeaway

Power is not only about who wins visible fights; it is about who sets the agenda and who shapes what people think they want. Institutions are the mechanism, and they are never neutral.

2.7 Exercises

Map the decision.
Pick a real decision, such as an API change, a layoff, a content-moderation rule. On a whiteboard, list (a) the visible decision-makers, (b) who set the agenda that made this the decision, and (c) whose preferences shaped what seemed “natural”. Which face of power is hardest to see, and why?
Three-faces audit.
Take a news story about a tech-policy fight. Find one example of each face of power, or explain which face is absent from the public account and why that absence matters.
Concentrated vs. diffuse.
List the stakeholders in a policy question (e.g., banning facial recognition in public spaces). Classify each as concentrated or diffuse, predict which will organize most effectively, and check your prediction against what actually happened.
Design the institution.
A team is choosing default privacy settings for a product. Write three different “rules of the game” (a default, a policy, a norm) that produce three different outcomes, and say who wins under each.
Path dependence hunt.
Find a feature, protocol, or company practice whose current form is explained more by history (“it’s always been done this way”) than by present need. Trace the lock-in and name who benefits from keeping it.
Quasi-government audit.
Pick one platform decision (a ban, a commission change, an API restriction) and compare it to a government decision. Who is the constituency? Where is the appeal? Which face of power is doing the work?
Incentive autopsy.
Take a case like Wells Fargo’s fake accounts. Trace the quota, the measurement, and the reward structure that produced the wrongdoing. Then redesign the institution so the bad behavior is no longer the rational response.
