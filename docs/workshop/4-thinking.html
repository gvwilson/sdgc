How People Actually Think

Greg Wilson

2026

Opening question

  • Why did you click the last thing you clicked?
  • Most online behavior is fast and automatic
  • That makes it predictable
  • Predictable is exploitable

Two systems [Kahneman2011]

  • System 1 is fast, automatic, and associative
  • System 2 is slow, effortful, and deliberative
  • Most online behavior runs on System 1
  • Biases are features, usually adaptive, but they are predictable

Heuristics and biases

  • Availability: what comes easily to mind
  • Anchoring: the first number or frame sticks
  • Confirmation: we seek and credit what we already believe
  • Representativeness: we judge by resemblance, not base rates

Loss aversion and discounting

  • Losses loom larger than gains
  • Taxi drivers work longer on bad days
  • Hyperbolic discounting: our future selves are strangers
  • Gym memberships and quitting smoking are the everyday cases

Identity and belonging [Tajfel1979]

  • The mere assignment of people to groups produces in-group favoritism
  • People hold beliefs partly to belong, not only to be right
  • Motivated reasoning: reasoning defends a conclusion rather than weighing evidence
  • When identity is at stake, facts become a lawyer, not a judge

Conformity and obedience [Asch1956]

  • Asch’s line studies: people matched a wrong answer to avoid standing alone
  • Milgram: sixty-five percent of subjects administered the maximum shock
  • Because a person in a lab coat told them to
  • Social context can overpower private judgment

The single dissenter

  • In Milgram-style setups, one dissenting confederate sharply reduces compliance
  • Conformity is fragile when someone models resistance
  • This matters for the change lesson later
  • One person going first changes what everyone else believes is possible

Moral foundations [Haidt2012]

  • Different audiences weight care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity differently
  • Persuasion that ignores this fails
  • Two reasonable people talk past each other about content moderation
  • Because they start from different foundations

Status beats income

  • People care about where they stand relative to others, not just absolute level
  • Norway’s public tax records widened the well-being gap by 29 percent [PerezTruglia2020]
  • Robert Frank’s local-rank finding: people care about position, not level
  • Public status comparisons are the strongest lever designers have

Stories beat data

  • Public-health campaigns use named people, not statistics
  • Industries fund data-heavy think tanks for the appearance of rigor
  • People want data but believe stories
  • A single vivid case outweighs a table of millions

The framing demonstration

  • Program A saves 200 people; Program B has a one-in-three chance to save all 600
  • Most people choose A
  • Reframe the same choice as deaths, and most choose the risky option
  • Nothing changed except how the outcomes were described

Anchoring in the wild

  • People spun a wheel rigged to land on 10 or 65, then guessed the share of African UN members
  • Those who saw 65 guessed about 45 percent higher
  • Prosecutors set high anchor charges; retailers display high “original” prices
  • The first number sticks, even when it is random

Nudges and the attention economy

  • Variable rewards, manufactured urgency, disguised ads, social proof, default nudges
  • Pension auto-enrollment took participation from 50-60 to 80-90 percent (UK 2012)
  • The tax letter “nine out of ten pay on time” raised compliance
  • A design choice is a psychological intervention, whether the designer admits it or not

Preference construction

  • Bernays reframed women smoking as feminist defiance to open the female market to tobacco
  • It worked
  • Recommendation systems are also preference-construction systems
  • The customer is never meant to arrive, because that would end the business model

Moral disengagement [Bandura1999]

  • Euphemisms let good people do harmful work without feeling like bad people
  • “User data as exhaust”
  • “Engagement optimization”
  • Corporate executives out-score Broadmoor patients on psychopathy measures
  • The Challenger disaster grew from “normalization of deviance,” not a single evil actor

Takeaway

  • People are not broken computers
  • They are social animals with fast, fallible, identity-driven cognition
  • Design that respects this is possible
  • Design that exploits it is the business model of the attention economy
  • “Just show people the facts” is not a change strategy
