Regulation

Greg Wilson

2026

Harm is not self-evident

  • What counts as harm is decided through contests of power, moral panic, and commercial interest
  • We do not first measure damage and then write a law
  • Alcohol kills hundreds of thousands a year and sits in supermarkets
  • Cannabis, with a lower measured harm profile, was criminalized for most of a century [Nutt2012]

Opening question

  • Which is more dangerous: alcohol or cannabis?
  • How do you know?
  • Does the law match your answer?
  • The gap between measured harm and legal treatment is the subject of this lesson

Four contested cases: sex

  • What was private versus criminal has been redrawn case by case, in someone’s interests
  • Sexual behavior between consenting adults was criminally regulated well into the twentieth century
  • Sodomy laws are a Victorian legal export from 1861 and 1885, spread through colonial law (Oscar Wilde)
  • They are not an ancient universal
  • The expansion of privacy was won, not discovered

Four contested cases: drugs

  • Enforcement of cannabis laws fell almost entirely on Black and Latino communities
  • Portugal’s 2001 decriminalization of personal possession was paired with treatment and harm reduction
  • It cut HIV transmission and overdose deaths without raising use [Hari2015]
  • Regulation tracks political economy and racial hierarchy, not harm

Four contested cases: guns

  • After Columbine, legislators went after violent video games
  • The evidence never supported that hypothesis
  • Cross-national comparisons point to gun availability and inequality as the real drivers
  • Motivated reasoning means even a trained scientist who owns a gun scrutinizes gun-violence studies more skeptically

Four contested cases: privacy

  • The historical unit of privacy was the household, not the person
  • Marital rape was legal in most US states until the 1970s and 1980s
  • What counts as a protected, private sphere is won and redrawn, not discovered [Igo2020]

The point of the four cases

  • We do not first measure harm and then regulate
  • We first decide who and what matters
  • Then we find or manufacture the harm that justifies the decision
  • Harm is contested, not measured

Two models of harm

  • Rare, dramatic, and attributable: the faulty valve that causes a boiler explosion
  • Diffuse, cumulative, and statistical: millions of small exposures with no single cause
  • The engineer’s default is the first model
  • The worst industrial harms are the second

The diffuse harm cases

  • Leaded gasoline lowered a generation’s IQs without any single tank causing measurable injury
  • No particular cigarette caused any particular cancer death [Brandt2007]
  • Social media harms are the diffuse kind, with a long probabilistic causal chain
  • That is why they are hard to regulate

The pollution model

  • Once harm is accepted as diffuse and cumulative, responsibility shifts
  • From proving individual causation to holding the emitter accountable for the aggregate
  • Engineers study pollution today because liability eventually forced them to
  • Not out of conscience

The non-identity problem

  • Some harms, especially to future people, cannot name an individual victim
  • If our choices change who is born, no particular future person can say we made them worse off
  • This is the philosophical twin of diffuse, cumulative harm
  • It explains why such harms are so easy to postpone and so hard to regulate

The regulatory toolkit

  • Standards and licensing
  • Disclosure requirements
  • Liability rules
  • Taxation and pricing
  • Structural remedies
  • The recurring failure mode: capture, where regulators come to serve the regulated

The uncertainty playbook: five steps

  • Fund scientists
  • Insist correlation is not causation
  • Call regulation an attack on freedom
  • Create neutral-sounding front groups
  • Delay [Oreskes2010]

The four phases

  • Denial
  • Uncertainty
  • The market argument
  • Acceptance
  • Compliance reliably turns out cheaper than predicted

The playbook in the wild

  • Tobacco ran this script for decades after Hill and Doll’s 1950 study [Brandt2007]
  • The same script ran for leaded gasoline, asbestos, and oxycontin
  • It is running now for social media and AI
  • GM’s response to Ralph Nader was private detectives digging for dirt, not fixing the car

Regulation that worked: pollution

  • The remedy: hold the emitter liable for the aggregate, not for a specific injury
  • The Cuyahoga River caught fire in 1969, for the thirteenth time since 1868
  • A visible disaster became leverage for enforceable rules
  • The ozone layer was protected before it cost lives

Minamata and the Rhine

  • Minamata: Chisso’s mercury poisoning, with cats as the ignored early warning
  • The lesson read backwards: waiting for a named victim is a trap
  • The 1986 Sandoz Rhine spill produced the binding Rhine Action Programme
  • A cross-border disaster was converted into enforceable rules

Regulation that worked: pharmaceuticals

  • Thalidomide was approved in West Germany in 1957
  • Frances Kelsey at the FDA refused approval because the safety evidence was insufficient
  • The birth defects where it was approved vindicated her
  • The result was pre-market approval and the precautionary principle

Kefauver-Harris (1962)

  • Before it, you could sell a drug in the US without proving it did anything
  • Pre-market approval puts the burden of proof on those introducing the risk
  • Nils Bohlin’s three-point belt: Volvo made the patent freely available to every manufacturer
  • The precautionary principle is the model for plausible, irreversible harms

The limits of a settlement

  • The 1998 Master Settlement Agreement recovered $206 billion from tobacco companies
  • It was a settlement, not a solution: it monetized the harm rather than removing it
  • A solution would have required a different instrument and an uncaptured regulator
  • The Triangle Shirtwaist owners were acquitted and collected the insurance

When regulators fail: capture

  • Capture is the recurring enemy
  • Regulators come to serve the regulated
  • The Monopolist’s Playbook: Standard Oil’s successors reconsolidated after the breakup
  • Rockefeller’s fortune increased as the subsidiary stock prices rose

Too big to jail

  • Wirecard: Germany’s regulator attacked the journalist instead of the fraud
  • Korean chaebol executives were convicted and then pardoned as too important to jail
  • “Too big to jail” is not a tech invention
  • ISDS is capture’s international cousin

Moral panic

  • The lineage: dime novels, comics, rock and roll, D&D, video games
  • Cross-national checks reveal how fragile most claims are
  • Japan and South Korea have high game use and low violence
  • The real harm in the video-game panic was loot boxes, not gunfire

Recap and takeaway

  • Social-media and AI harms are pollution-model harms
  • The trap is moral-panic framing such as “ban the kids’ phones”
  • The actual harms: attention extraction, addictive design, discrimination, surveillance
  • Regulating well means diagnosing the actual harm and defending the regulator from capture
