Power and Institutions

Greg Wilson

2026

Opening question

  • Think of a product decision at your company
  • Who actually decided it?
  • Whose interests won?
  • Lasswell defined politics in 1936 as “who gets what, when, how”

Three faces of power [Lukes2021]

  • Face one: who wins when there is a visible decision
  • Face two: who controls what gets onto the agenda in the first place
  • Face three: who shapes what people even want, so some options never occur to anyone
  • Power is not only about winning visible fights

Face one: the visible decision

  • Who wins the vote, the launch review, the promotion panel
  • Everyone can see this face, so it gets the most attention
  • But the menu was usually fixed before the meeting started
  • The announced decision is the last act, not the first

Face two: the agenda

  • Who decides what goes on the roadmap, the API surface, the meeting agenda
  • A “neutral” default or a “technical” constraint is usually agenda-setting
  • Absence from the agenda is a form of power
  • If a problem is never scheduled, it is never decided

Face three: preference-shaping

  • Who shapes what feels natural, obvious, or inevitable
  • “We don’t do politics here” is the third face’s catchphrase
  • It usually means the winners already have what they want
  • And they can describe their arrangement as natural

Institutions are the rules of the game [North1990]

  • Formal rules: laws and contracts
  • Informal constraints: norms, conventions, taboos
  • Institutions always allocate advantage; they are never neutral
  • They structure interaction before any individual makes a choice

Software is an institution

  • Default settings, permission models, and rate limits are rules
  • Each one allocates advantage to someone
  • The corporation is also an institution, and its form is a choice
  • Founders and venture capitalists benefit most from the conventional structure

A design decision is a governance decision

  • The 1886 US railroad gauge change was a market weapon, not a technical necessity
    • Southern carriers profited from reloading freight at the gauge boundary
  • A design choice that looked technical was actually a tariff
  • An employer controls income; an infrastructure provider controls the conditions of earning

Tithing and excommunication

  • The App Store’s 30 percent cut is a tithe
  • Deplatforming is excommunication from the infrastructure needed to do business
  • A suspended WeChat account removes civic and commercial life, not just chat
  • Governance is whoever controls the infrastructure

Incentives produce the wrongdoing

  • Wells Fargo fired 5,300 staff over fake accounts, under “eight is great”
  • Mass wrongdoing flows from impossible quotas, not from bad individuals
  • The institution produced the behavior the designers later blamed on employees

Self-fulfilling design

  • Terms of service written for adversaries produce the distrust they assume
  • Moderation that treats everyone as a suspect does the same
  • Contrast with Ostrom’s governed commons, where rules assume cooperation
  • Institutions teach people how to behave, then reward what they teach

Platforms as quasi-governments

  • eBay adjudicates disputes
  • App Store review is the only appeal
  • Facebook makes de facto regulatory decisions in other countries
  • The yakuza delivered 2011 Tohoku disaster relief faster than the state

The company town

  • George Pullman cut wages in 1893 while holding rents fixed
    • Workers could not cut other expenses because the company controlled those too
  • The 1894 Pullman Strike paralyzed rail traffic and required federal troops
  • Total control eventually produces resistance

Legibility [Scott1998]

  • Surnames, house numbers, and passports exist so the state can tax, draft, and police
  • South Africa’s pass laws became the world’s first biometric register in 1986
  • Standardization is an instrument of administration, not a neutral convenience
  • The state standardizes people so they can be governed

Philanthrocapitalism

  • Donor-advised funds and the Giving Pledge convert taxable wealth into controlled endowments
  • Roughly $230 billion sits in donor-advised funds
  • The Gates Foundation was at one point the WHO’s second-largest funder
  • Private money buys policy influence while wearing the language of charity

Collective action [Olson1965]

  • It is easier to organize a small group with a large stake than a large group with a small stake
  • This is why producers beat consumers and platforms beat users
  • Ad-tech firms are few, organized, and rich; users are many, unorganized, and each has a small stake
  • This single asymmetry predicts much of tech policy

The winning coalition

  • Selectorate theory: leaders distribute to the small coalition that keeps them in power
  • Not to the whole population, and not to the whole “family”
  • Musk’s “hardcore” email let the remaining staff confirm loyalty while others resigned
  • Pfeffer found political skill predicts advancement better than technical competence

Recap

  • Power is about who sets the agenda and who shapes what people think they want
  • Institutions are the mechanism, and they are never neutral
  • Path dependence: early choices lock in later outcomes even after the original reasons disappear
  • The enclosure movement is the same lesson: the winners wrote the rules through Parliament
