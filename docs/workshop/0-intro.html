Introduction

Greg Wilson

2026

What this workshop is about

  • Two days for people with programming backgrounds
  • Focus: the harms of social media and AI, and how to regulate them
  • Goal: turn claims into tools you can actually use
  • Structure: seven lessons, each with one or two exercises

At a glance

  • Length: two days, roughly twelve to fourteen hours of teaching
  • Audience: people with programming backgrounds
  • Format: seven lessons of sixty to ninety minutes
  • Each lesson is followed by one or two short exercises

Our stance (part 1)

  • Power is distributed very unequally, and much that looks neutral or technical is a rule favoring some people over others
  • Inequality is mostly the product of policy choices and inherited advantage, not the natural sorting of talent
  • Markets are human institutions, not laws of nature

Our stance (part 2)

  • What counts as harm is decided by people, not measured
  • Alcohol, cannabis, sex, and guns are not regulated in proportion to the damage they do
  • Regulation of food, medicine, air, water, cars, and tobacco has repeatedly protected the public
  • Failures come from regulatory capture and weak enforcement, not the impossibility of governing markets

Our stance (part 3)

  • Change is possible
  • Change is usually won by organized collective action, not persuasion alone
  • Technologists have unusual leverage in the fights now under way

Learning outcomes (part 1)

  • Distinguish structural explanations from individual ones, and why individual ones are the tech default
  • Identify who benefits and who bears the cost, especially concentrated benefits and diffuse costs
  • Recognize market failures and name the regulatory instruments available
  • Describe how cognitive biases, identity, and group belonging shape belief

Learning outcomes (part 2)

  • Explain how a society decides what counts as harm, and the rare versus diffuse models of harm
  • Give examples of regulation that worked and failed, and the mechanisms that explain the difference
  • Explain how media and platforms frame issues and shape what is debatable
  • Connect harms of social media and AI to each lesson and sketch realistic responses

How to run this

  • Pick one or two exercises per lesson; do not try to run them all
  • Exercises are the assessment; do not skip the debriefs
  • Use groupwork and think-pair-share to get more voices into the room
  • Keep it concrete: ask what each idea looks like in a product or a law
  • Do not manufacture consensus; ask what would change someone’s mind
  • Keep descriptive claims separate from normative ones

Materials

  • A whiteboard or shared document for each group
  • Sticky notes
  • Breakout spaces
